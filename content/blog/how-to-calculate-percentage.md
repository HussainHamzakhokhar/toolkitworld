Percentages are everywhere: discounts, exam scores, interest rates, tips, survey results and nutrition labels. The good news is that almost every percentage question uses one simple idea, and three short formulas cover nearly all of them. This guide explains each one with worked examples, then covers the mistakes people make most often.

## What a percentage actually means

"Per cent" means "per hundred". So 35% means 35 out of every 100, which you can also write as the fraction 35/100 or the decimal 0.35. Converting between these forms is the key skill:

- **Percent to decimal:** divide by 100. 35% → 0.35, 7% → 0.07, 120% → 1.2.
- **Decimal to percent:** multiply by 100. 0.6 → 60%, 0.045 → 4.5%.

Once a percentage is a decimal, you can multiply with it like any other number. That is all most calculations need.

## Formula 1: Finding a percentage of a number

**Question type:** "What is P% of Y?"

> Result = (P ÷ 100) × Y

**Example:** What is 15% of 240?

1. Convert 15% to a decimal: 15 ÷ 100 = 0.15
2. Multiply: 0.15 × 240 = 36

So 15% of 240 is **36**.

A useful mental shortcut: 10% of any number is that number divided by 10. From there, 5% is half of 10%, and 20% is double. For 15% of 240: 10% is 24, 5% is 12, so 15% is 36.

## Formula 2: What percent one number is of another

**Question type:** "X is what percent of Y?"

> Percentage = (X ÷ Y) × 100

**Example:** You answered 42 out of 56 questions correctly. What is your score as a percentage?

1. Divide the part by the whole: 42 ÷ 56 = 0.75
2. Multiply by 100: 0.75 × 100 = 75%

The most common mistake here is dividing the wrong way round. The number after "of" — the whole — always goes on the bottom.

## Formula 3: Finding the whole from a percentage

**Question type:** "X is P% of what number?"

> Whole = X ÷ (P ÷ 100)

**Example:** A deposit of 90 is 30% of the total price. What is the total?

1. Convert 30% to a decimal: 0.30
2. Divide: 90 ÷ 0.30 = 300

The total price is **300**. This is the same reasoning used to find an original price before a discount, which our [Discount Calculator](/calculators/discount-calculator/) does for you.

## Percentage change: increases and decreases

When a value moves from an old number to a new one, the percentage change compares the difference to the **original** value:

> Change % = (New − Original) ÷ Original × 100

If a monthly bill rises from 80 to 92, the change is (92 − 80) ÷ 80 × 100 = **15% increase**. If it falls from 92 back to 80, the change is (80 − 92) ÷ 92 × 100 ≈ **13.04% decrease**. The two percentages are different because the starting value is different each time.

We go much deeper into this, including percentage points and reverse calculations, in [How to Calculate Percentage Increase](/blog/how-to-calculate-percentage-increase/).

## Adding or subtracting a percentage

To increase a number by P%, multiply by (1 + P ÷ 100). To decrease it, multiply by (1 − P ÷ 100).

- Add 8% tax to 50: 50 × 1.08 = 54
- Take 25% off 50: 50 × 0.75 = 37.5

This "multiplier" method is faster than working out the percentage and adding it separately, and it makes repeated changes easy: two years of 5% growth is × 1.05 × 1.05 = × 1.1025, which is 10.25% in total — not 10%.

## Common mistakes to avoid

1. **Using the wrong base.** "Prices rose 20% and then fell 20%" does not return you to the start. 100 → 120 → 96, because the 20% fall is taken from 120.
2. **Adding percentages that apply in sequence.** A 30% discount followed by an extra 10% off is 37% off in total, not 40%.
3. **Confusing percent with percentage points.** An interest rate going from 2% to 3% is a rise of 1 percentage point, but a 50% increase in the rate.
4. **Dividing by zero.** You cannot calculate what percentage something is of 0, or a percentage change from 0. A good calculator should say so instead of showing a number.

## Frequently asked questions

### How do I calculate a percentage on a phone calculator?

Multiply by the percentage and divide by 100. For 18% of 65, type 65 × 18 ÷ 100 = 11.7. Many phone calculators also have a % key, but its behaviour varies, so the multiply-and-divide method is more reliable.

### How do I calculate a percentage of a total in a spreadsheet?

Divide the part by the total and format the cell as a percentage. For example `=B2/B10`, then apply percentage formatting. Without formatting, multiply by 100.

### Can a percentage be over 100?

Yes. 150% of 40 is 60, and if something grows from 40 to 100 that is a 150% increase. Only when a percentage describes a share of a whole — like a test score — is it limited to 100%.

### What is 1% of a number?

Divide the number by 100. 1% of 3,500 is 35. Multiply that by any percentage you need: 7% of 3,500 is 7 × 35 = 245.

## Conclusion

Most percentage questions come down to three formulas: a percentage *of* a number, what percent one number *is of* another, and finding the whole from a part. Add percentage change and the multiplier method, keep track of which number is the base, and you can handle shopping, finance, grades and statistics with confidence.
