Prices go up, salaries rise, followers grow and costs fall. Describing those changes as a percentage makes them easy to compare: a rise from 20 to 25 and from 2,000 to 2,500 are both 25% increases. This guide explains the percentage change formula, works through real-world examples, and clears up the classic confusion between percent and percentage points.

## The percentage increase formula

> Percentage increase = (New value − Original value) ÷ Original value × 100

In words: find how much the value went up, then express that rise as a share of where it started.

**Example: a price rise.** A train ticket goes from 40 to 46.

1. Increase: 46 − 40 = 6
2. Divide by the original: 6 ÷ 40 = 0.15
3. Multiply by 100: **15% increase**

## The percentage decrease formula

A decrease uses the same idea, measured against the original value:

> Percentage decrease = (Original value − New value) ÷ Original value × 100

**Example: a smaller energy bill.** Your bill falls from 125 to 100.

1. Decrease: 125 − 100 = 25
2. Divide by the original: 25 ÷ 125 = 0.2
3. Multiply by 100: **20% decrease**

If you use the general "change" formula (new − original) for a decrease, you simply get a negative result: (100 − 125) ÷ 125 × 100 = −20%.

## Why the base matters so much

The same gap gives different percentages depending on the direction:

- From 100 to 125 is a **25% increase**.
- From 125 back to 100 is a **20% decrease**.

That is why a 50% fall followed by a 50% rise does not get you back to the start: 200 → 100 → 150. The rise is calculated from the smaller number. Investors know this well — after losing 50%, you need a 100% gain to break even.

## Real-world examples

### Salary increase

Your salary rises from 42,000 to 45,150. The increase is 3,150, and 3,150 ÷ 42,000 × 100 = **7.5%**. Comparing that with the inflation rate tells you whether your pay rose in real terms.

### Population growth

A town grows from 18,400 to 19,780 people. The change is 1,380, so the growth is 1,380 ÷ 18,400 × 100 = **7.5%**.

### Growth of more than 100%

A small online shop's monthly orders grow from 80 to 260. The increase is 180, and 180 ÷ 80 × 100 = **225%**. Percentage increases have no upper limit; "doubling" is a 100% increase, "tripling" is 200%.

## Going backwards: finding the original value

Sometimes you know the new value and the percentage, and need the starting point.

> Original = New ÷ (1 + increase ÷ 100)

A jacket costs 69 after a 15% price rise. The old price was 69 ÷ 1.15 = **60**. A common mistake is to subtract 15% of 69 (giving 58.65) — but the 15% was calculated on the original, not the new price.

For decreases, divide by (1 − decrease ÷ 100) instead. The [Discount Calculator](/calculators/discount-calculator/) has a dedicated "Original price" mode for this.

## Percent vs percentage points

When the thing that changes is itself a percentage — an interest rate, a tax rate, an unemployment rate, a vote share — there are two ways to describe the change:

- **Percentage points** describe the simple difference: an interest rate going from 4% to 5% has risen by **1 percentage point**.
- **Percent** describes the relative change: that same move is a **25% increase** in the rate, because 1 ÷ 4 = 0.25.

News reports sometimes mix these up, which can make a change sound much bigger or smaller than it really is. When reading statistics, check which one is meant.

## Percentage change vs percentage difference

Percentage change has a clear "before" and "after". When you are comparing two things without a direction — say, prices in two different shops — use the **percentage difference**, which divides by the average of the two values:

> Percentage difference = |A − B| ÷ ((A + B) ÷ 2) × 100

Prices of 45 and 55 differ by 10, and their average is 50, so the percentage difference is **20%**, whichever shop you start from.

## Frequently asked questions

### How do I calculate percentage increase in Excel or Google Sheets?

Use `=(B2-A2)/A2` where A2 is the original value and B2 is the new value, then format the cell as a percentage.

### What if the original value is negative?

Divide by the absolute value of the original. Going from −50 to −25 is then a 50% increase. Interpret such results with care, as they can be counter-intuitive.

### Is a percentage increase the same as a growth rate?

For one period, yes. Over several periods, analysts often use the compound annual growth rate (CAGR) instead, which accounts for growth building on growth.

## Conclusion

Percentage increase and decrease both compare a change to the **original** value. Keep track of the base, use multipliers to go backwards, and distinguish percent from percentage points, and you will read and calculate changes accurately.
